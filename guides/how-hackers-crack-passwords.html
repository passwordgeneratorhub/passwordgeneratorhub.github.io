How Hackers Crack Passwords (and How to Slow Them Down)

Dictionary attacks, rainbow tables, credential stuffing — the methods attackers use, explained without jargon, and the defences that work.

Password cracking sounds like something from a film: a hacker typing furiously while a progress bar fills. The reality is less dramatic and more effective. Attackers use automated tools, huge lists of stolen passwords, and powerful computers to try millions of guesses per second — and they succeed far more often than most people realise, because most passwords are predictable. Understanding how they do it is the best way to understand why the standard advice — long, random, unique passwords — actually works. None of these attacks is magic; each one has a specific defence.

Dictionary attacks: trying every word

The simplest attack is the dictionary attack: the attacker tries every word in a dictionary, then every common password, then every variation of those words with numbers and symbols added. Modern dictionaries contain billions of entries, including every password ever leaked in a breach, every name, every sports team, and every keyboard pattern. A password like sunshine123 or liverpool2024 falls to a dictionary attack in seconds, because those words and patterns are in the lists. The defence is randomness: a password that is not a word, a name, or a pattern cannot be found in a dictionary, no matter how large the dictionary is.

Brute force: trying everything

If the dictionary fails, an attacker can try brute force — every possible combination, one by one. This works, but it is slow: the number of combinations grows exponentially with length, so an 8-character password of mixed characters can be brute-forced in hours by a powerful computer, while a 16-character one would take centuries. That is why length matters so much. Brute force is also limited by speed: an attacker guessing against a live website is slowed by rate limits and lockouts, but an attacker with a stolen password database can guess offline at billions of tries per second. The defence is length and uniqueness — a long password that appears in no database is safe from both.

Rainbow tables and fast hashes

Websites do not store your password; they store a hash, a scrambled fingerprint of it. But if the hashing is fast and unsalted, an attacker can precompute the hashes of billions of passwords — a rainbow table — and look up your hash in seconds. This is why the choice of hashing matters and why breaches of old systems are so dangerous. Modern systems use slow, salted hashing, which makes each guess expensive and each password unique, defeating rainbow tables. You cannot control how a website stores your password, which is exactly why you should use a unique password for every site: if one site stores passwords badly and is breached, only that account is at risk.

Credential stuffing and phishing

The most successful attacks do not crack passwords at all — they reuse them or steal them. Credential stuffing tests passwords leaked from one breach against hundreds of other sites, exploiting reuse. Phishing tricks you into typing your password into a fake login page, bypassing all its strength. Both are defeated by habits, not by password complexity: unique passwords stop credential stuffing, and two-factor authentication stops both, because a stolen password alone is not enough to log in. Checking the address bar before typing a password, and never following login links from emails, closes the phishing route.

What actually slows attackers down

Every defence works by making guessing more expensive: length multiplies the combinations, randomness defeats dictionaries, uniqueness limits the damage of a breach, slow hashing makes each guess costly, rate limiting slows online guessing, and two-factor authentication makes a guessed password useless on its own. No single measure is enough, but together they make attacking your accounts more expensive than it is worth. Attackers are opportunists — they move on to easier targets. Your goal is not to be uncrackable in theory; it is to be expensive enough in practice that they never bother trying.

Frequently asked questions

How long would it take to crack my password?

It depends on length and randomness. An 8-character mixed password may fall in hours offline; a 16-character random one would take centuries. Use our entropy calculator to estimate yours.

Are password-cracking tools available to anyone?

Yes, the tools are public. The defence is not secrecy about the tools, but passwords that resist them: long, random and unique.

Does two-factor authentication stop password cracking?

Effectively, yes. Even if an attacker guesses your password, they still need your second factor to log in.
