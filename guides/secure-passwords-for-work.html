Password Security at Work: A Guide for Employees

Work accounts are prime targets. Here is how to keep company logins safe without slowing yourself down.

Work accounts are among the most valuable targets an attacker can find: a single compromised employee login can open a company's email, files, customer data and internal systems. At the same time, employees are busy, and security rules that slow people down get worked around — passwords written on monitors, shared logins, and shortcuts that undo the protection. Good workplace password security is about making the safe way also the easy way. Whether you are an employee following the rules or a small-business owner writing them, these practices protect the organisation without making the workday miserable.

Use a business password manager

The single best investment a workplace can make is a business password manager with shared vaults. It lets teams share logins securely without ever seeing the password itself, and it lets administrators revoke access instantly when someone leaves. Employees stop reusing personal passwords at work, stop emailing credentials to each other, and stop writing passwords down. If your employer provides one, use it for every work account. If they do not, ask for one — it costs far less than a single breach — and in the meantime use your personal manager's work vault, keeping work and personal credentials separate.

Never share logins by email or chat

Sending a password in an email or a chat message creates a permanent copy in inboxes, backups and chat logs, where it can be found months later by anyone with access. It also trains people to trust credential messages, which is exactly how phishing works. If a colleague needs access to a shared account, share it through the password manager, which encrypts the credential and lets you revoke it later. If there is no manager, change the password after the other person has used it. And never share your personal login — the one tied to your name — with anyone, including your manager or IT support. Legitimate support staff never need your password.

Treat phishing as a work hazard

Most workplace breaches start with a phishing email, not a guessed password: a fake invoice, a fake password-expiry warning, or a message pretending to be the boss asking for an urgent transfer. The defence is habit, not cleverness. Check the sender's real address before clicking, hover over links to see where they lead, and be suspicious of any message that creates urgency or asks for credentials. If a message claims your password has expired, go to the service directly by typing its address, never through the link in the message. Report suspicious messages to IT instead of deleting them quietly — your report may protect colleagues who received the same email.

Leaving, joining and changing roles

Password hygiene matters most at moments of change. When you join, set up unique passwords and two-factor authentication for every work system on day one, and do not reuse your personal passwords. When you change roles, hand over shared credentials through the manager and remove your access to systems you no longer need. When you leave, your employer should revoke your accounts promptly, and you should remove work credentials from your personal devices. Former employees with lingering access are a common and entirely preventable cause of breaches, so treat offboarding as a security task, not just paperwork.

What managers should do differently

If you set policy, stop requiring password changes every ninety days — modern guidance says to change on compromise, not on a schedule, because forced rotation produces weaker passwords. Require two-factor authentication for email and remote access, provide a password manager, and give people a way to report phishing without blame. Measure the things that matter: how many accounts have unique passwords, how many have two-factor authentication, and how quickly compromised credentials are revoked. A culture where people can admit they clicked a suspicious link is worth more than any password rule, because early reporting stops a breach while it is still small.

Frequently asked questions

Should I use my personal password manager for work?

Only if your employer has no business manager, and keep work credentials in a separate vault. A business manager is better because access can be revoked when you leave.

What should I do if I clicked a phishing link at work?

Tell IT immediately. Quick reporting limits the damage, and a blameless culture is what makes people report fast.

Do I need two-factor authentication on work accounts?

Yes, especially for email and remote access. It is the single most effective defence against stolen work credentials.
