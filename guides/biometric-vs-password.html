Biometrics vs Passwords: Which Protects You Better?

Your fingerprint cannot be forgotten — but it also cannot be changed. How biometrics and passwords compare, and how to use both.

Fingerprint readers and face recognition have made unlocking a phone fast and convenient, and many people now wonder whether biometrics have made passwords obsolete. They have not, and they probably will not. Biometrics and passwords protect you in different ways, with different strengths and different weaknesses, and the best security uses both. Understanding the trade-offs helps you decide when to trust your face or finger, when to insist on a password, and what to do when biometrics go wrong.

What biometrics do well

Biometrics are convenient: you always carry them, you cannot forget them, and unlocking with a finger or a glance takes a second. They are also resistant to the most common password attacks — nobody can phish your fingerprint, guess it from a dictionary, or steal it in a database breach and use it on another site. Modern phones store biometric data in a secure chip on the device and never send it to a server, so the fingerprint itself never travels over the internet. For day-to-day unlocking, biometrics are both faster and safer than typing a short PIN or a weak password dozens of times a day.

What biometrics do badly

The fundamental weakness of biometrics is that you cannot change them. If a password leaks, you change it; if a fingerprint is copied, you have only nine others, and you cannot change your face at all. Biometrics can also be defeated in ways passwords cannot: a fingerprint can be lifted from a glass, a face can be photographed, and identical twins or family members can sometimes unlock each other's devices. There is also a legal difference in many countries: in some places, police can compel you to unlock a device with your fingerprint, but cannot compel you to reveal a password from memory. And biometrics fail for practical reasons too — wet fingers, face masks, injuries and ageing all cause false rejections.

How phones actually combine them

In practice, phones do not replace passwords with biometrics; they layer them. Your fingerprint or face unlocks the device quickly, but the underlying encryption key is still protected by your passcode, and the phone demands the passcode after a restart, after a period of inactivity, or after several failed biometric attempts. Banking apps often require the passcode or a fresh biometric check for large transactions. This design gets the best of both worlds: convenience for routine unlocking and the revocability of a password for the underlying security. It also means your passcode matters more, not less — a weak passcode undermines the whole system, because anyone who guesses it can bypass the fingerprint entirely.

Where passwords still win

Passwords win wherever revocation, secrecy and remote use matter. You can change a password after a breach, share one securely and revoke it, use one from any device in the world, and keep it entirely in your head. Biometrics cannot do any of those things. That is why your most important accounts — email, banking, password manager — should still be protected by a strong unique password plus two-factor authentication, even if your phone unlocks with your face. Biometrics are an excellent lock for a device you carry; they are a poor replacement for the password that guards your digital identity.

Practical advice for everyday use

Use biometrics to unlock your phone — it is safer than a short PIN typed in public. But set a strong alphanumeric passcode as the fallback, not 1234, and make sure the phone requires it after restarts. Register only your own fingers and face, and remove old registrations when you sell or give away a device. For sensitive apps, enable the extra step the app offers, such as requiring the passcode for payments. And remember that biometrics are a convenience layer on top of a password, not a substitute for one: the password underneath is what ultimately protects you, so make it strong and unique.

Frequently asked questions

Can someone steal my fingerprint from my phone?

Modern phones store fingerprint data in a secure chip and never upload it, so a remote attacker cannot steal it. A physical copy from a surface is possible but rare.

Are biometrics safer than a password?

For unlocking a phone, yes. For protecting an online account, no — a strong unique password plus two-factor authentication is still the better choice.

What happens if my fingerprint stops working?

Your passcode is the fallback. This is another reason to choose a strong passcode rather than a simple PIN.
