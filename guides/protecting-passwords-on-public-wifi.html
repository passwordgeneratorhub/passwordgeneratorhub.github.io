How to Protect Your Passwords on Public Wi-Fi

Free airport and cafe Wi-Fi is convenient — and a favourite hunting ground for password thieves. Stay safe with these habits.

Public Wi-Fi is one of the great conveniences of modern travel: free internet in airports, cafes, hotels and trains. It is also one of the easiest places for an attacker to steal a password. On an open network, traffic can be observed, fake hotspots can impersonate the real one, and an attacker on the same network can try to intercept logins. The good news is that modern websites and a few simple habits make public Wi-Fi far safer than it used to be. You do not need to avoid it entirely; you need to use it wisely.

How attacks on public Wi-Fi actually work

The classic attack is the evil twin: an attacker sets up a hotspot named Free_Airport_WiFi or CoffeeShop_Guest, and your device connects to it instead of the real network. Once connected, the attacker can see unencrypted traffic and redirect you to fake login pages. A second attack is sniffing, where an attacker on the same network watches traffic that is not encrypted. A third is session hijacking, where an attacker steals a logged-in session cookie. All three rely on the same weakness: traffic that is not properly encrypted. Modern sites use HTTPS, which encrypts the connection between your browser and the site, but a fake hotspot can still show you a convincing fake page and ask you to type your password into it.

Use a VPN when it matters

A virtual private network encrypts all traffic between your device and the VPN server, so anyone watching the local Wi-Fi sees only scrambled data. A reputable paid VPN is the most practical protection on public networks, especially if you need to log in to email, banking or work systems. Free VPNs are a mixed bag: some are legitimate, but others log and sell your browsing data, which defeats the purpose. If you do not have a VPN, you can still use public Wi-Fi safely for low-risk browsing, but avoid logging into important accounts. Many employers provide a VPN for work devices — turn it on before doing anything sensitive.

Check for HTTPS and beware of warnings

Before typing any password, check that the address starts with https and that your browser shows a padlock. If the browser warns that the connection is not private or the certificate is invalid, stop — do not click through the warning to log in. That warning can mean an attacker is intercepting the connection. The same applies to captive portals, the login pages that hotels and airports show before granting internet access: they are normal, but never type your email or banking password into a captive portal. Only your Wi-Fi access credentials belong there, and only if you trust the venue. When in doubt, ask staff for the exact network name.

Turn off auto-join and sharing

Your phone helpfully remembers networks and reconnects automatically, which is exactly what an evil-twin attack exploits: an attacker broadcasts a familiar name and your phone joins without asking. Turn off auto-join for public networks, and forget networks you no longer use. Also turn off file sharing, AirDrop, and printer sharing while on public Wi-Fi, so other devices on the network cannot reach yours. Keep your operating system and browser updated, because updates patch the vulnerabilities that network attacks exploit. And set your device to treat public networks as untrusted — most systems ask this when you first connect.

What to do after using public Wi-Fi

After a trip, a quick check costs little and catches problems early. Review your important accounts for unfamiliar logins or devices, and sign out of sessions you do not recognise. If you logged into anything sensitive on a network you now distrust, change those passwords from a trusted connection and make sure two-factor authentication is on. If your browser warned you about a certificate while travelling, treat it seriously and change the passwords you used around that time. These steps take ten minutes and turn a risky connection into a minor incident at worst. The real lesson is not to fear public Wi-Fi, but to assume it is hostile and behave accordingly.

Frequently asked questions

Is public Wi-Fi safe for banking?

Avoid logging into banking on open Wi-Fi unless you are using a VPN. Your bank's app uses encryption, but a VPN adds a layer against fake hotspots and interception.

Does HTTPS alone protect me on public Wi-Fi?

Mostly. HTTPS encrypts your login, but it cannot protect you from a fake login page that looks identical to the real one.

Should I use a free VPN on public Wi-Fi?

Be cautious. Some free VPNs sell your data. A reputable paid VPN or your employer's VPN is a safer choice.
