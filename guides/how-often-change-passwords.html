How Often Should You Change Your Passwords?

The old advice to change passwords every 90 days is outdated. Here is what modern guidance actually says.

For years, workplaces and websites told everyone to change their passwords every month or every ninety days. The advice sounded sensible, but it backfired: people who must change passwords constantly choose weaker ones, write them down, or make tiny predictable changes like turning Password1 into Password2. Modern security guidance has reversed course. Today, the official advice from standards bodies is to change a password only when there is a reason — a breach, a suspected compromise, or a shared password — and otherwise to leave a strong, unique password alone. This guide explains when to change, when not to, and what to do instead.

Why forced rotation made passwords weaker

When people are forced to invent a new password every few weeks, they optimise for memorability, not security. Research found that forced changes led to shorter passwords, more reuse, and predictable patterns like incrementing a digit or adding a season name. Attackers know these patterns and try them first when an old password leaks. The policy also created a false sense of security: a changed password felt safe even when it was weak, while a strong unique password that never changed was treated as a problem. That is why the US National Institute of Standards and Technology removed mandatory periodic changes from its guidance, and why many companies and governments have followed.

When you should change a password immediately

Change a password at once if the service tells you it was breached, if you receive a breach alert for that account, if you notice logins you do not recognise, or if you entered the password on a phishing site. Change it too if you shared it with someone who no longer needs it — a former colleague, a housemate, an ex-partner — or if you typed it on a shared or public computer. If you used the same password on another site that was breached, change it on every site where it was used. In all of these cases, speed matters: an attacker with a leaked password typically tries it within hours, so change it the same day you learn of the problem.

When you can leave a password alone

If a password is long, random, unique to one account, stored in a password manager, and protected by two-factor authentication, there is no benefit in changing it on a schedule. It is not getting weaker with age; guessing attacks do not care how old a password is, only how many combinations it has. Leave it alone and spend the effort on things that actually improve security, such as turning on two-factor authentication, removing old accounts you no longer use, and checking breach alerts. The exception is a password you created before you knew better — a short, reused or guessable one. Change those once, properly, and then stop rotating them.

A practical review schedule that works

Instead of rotating passwords on a calendar, review your security twice a year. Check your password manager for reused or weak passwords and fix the worst ones. Check breach alerts for your email address. Remove accounts you no longer use, because a forgotten account with a reused password is a quiet risk. Check that two-factor authentication is still on for your important accounts, and that your recovery email and phone number are current. This twice-yearly review takes less than an hour and catches real problems, whereas quarterly forced changes mostly create new weak passwords.

What about work policies that still demand rotation?

Some employers and older systems still require password changes every ninety days, and you cannot always change the policy yourself. In that case, use a password manager to generate a fresh random password each time, so the change is painless and the new password is genuinely strong. If you must remember it, use a new passphrase each time rather than incrementing the old one. You can also ask your IT team whether the policy has been reviewed recently — many organisations have dropped rotation after learning the modern guidance, and a polite question with a link to the current standards can start that conversation.

Frequently asked questions

Do I still need to change my password every 90 days?

No, not unless there is a reason such as a breach. Modern guidance says to change a password when it is compromised, not on a schedule.

How often should I review my passwords?

Twice a year is enough for most people: fix weak or reused passwords, check breach alerts, and confirm two-factor authentication is on.

Does an old password get weaker over time?

No. A strong password does not decay; what matters is its length, randomness, uniqueness, and whether it has leaked.
