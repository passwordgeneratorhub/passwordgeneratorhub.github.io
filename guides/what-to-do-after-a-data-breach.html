What to Do After a Data Breach Exposes Your Password

Your password is in a leak. Do not panic — act in the right order, and the damage stops here.

Learning that your password appeared in a data breach is unpleasant, but it is also common and, in most cases, fixable in under an hour. Breaches happen to large and careful companies alike, and your password may have leaked through no fault of your own. What matters is what you do next, and the order you do it in. Acting quickly and methodically protects your accounts; ignoring the news and hoping for the best is what turns a small leak into a stolen identity. This guide is the checklist to follow the moment you get a breach alert.

Step one: change the breached password

Start with the account that was breached. Change its password immediately, from a device you trust, and make the new password long, random and unique — use a password manager to generate it. Do not reuse the old password with a small change, because attackers try exactly those variations. If you used the same password anywhere else, change it on every one of those accounts too, starting with email and banking. This is the most important step and the most urgent: automated attacks test leaked passwords within hours of a breach becoming public, so the same day matters far more than the same week.

Step two: secure your email first

If the breached account is your email, or if your email password was the same as the breached one, your email is the priority. Email controls password resets for nearly everything, so an attacker in your inbox can reset your bank, your shopping and your social media one by one. Change the email password, turn on two-factor authentication, and check the account for changes: forwarding rules you did not set, unfamiliar recovery addresses, and sent messages you did not write. Attackers often set up silent forwarding so they keep receiving your mail even after you change the password, so look for rules and filters, not just the password.

Step three: check for unauthorised activity

Go through the accounts that shared the password and look for anything unfamiliar: new devices in the security settings, logins from places you have never been, purchases you did not make, messages you did not send, and changed details such as a new phone number or recovery email. Most services show recent activity in their security settings. If you find unauthorised activity, sign out of all sessions — most services offer a sign-out-everywhere button — and change the password again. For financial accounts, check recent transactions and tell your bank if anything looks wrong; they can reverse fraudulent charges and watch the account.

Step four: turn on two-factor authentication

A breach is the best possible reminder to enable two-factor authentication everywhere it matters, because it makes a leaked password useless on its own. Set it up with an authenticator app where possible, and save the recovery codes the service gives you. Do this for email, banking, cloud storage, social media and any shopping site that stores your card. It takes a few minutes per account and is the single most effective defence against the next breach, because your password will leak again someday — the question is whether the leak alone is enough to get in.

Step five: watch for phishing and identity theft

After a breach, expect phishing. Attackers know your name, email and possibly your address, so they can write convincing messages pretending to be the breached company, your bank, or a delivery service. Do not click links in emails about the breach; go to the company's site directly. Watch for password-reset emails you did not request — they mean someone is trying to get into your accounts. If the breach included sensitive data such as your address, date of birth or identity documents, consider a credit freeze or fraud alert with the credit bureaus in your country, and keep an eye on your financial statements for the next few months. Most breach victims who act quickly suffer no lasting harm.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if my password was in a breach?

Search your email on Have I Been Pwned, or check your browser's or password manager's breach alerts.

Should I change all my passwords after a breach?

Change the breached one and every account that shared it. If you have unique passwords everywhere, only the breached account needs changing.

What if the breach included my credit card?

Tell your bank immediately and ask for a replacement card. Monitor statements and consider a fraud alert.
