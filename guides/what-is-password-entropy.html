What Is Password Entropy and Why Does It Matter?

Entropy is the number that tells you how hard a password is to guess. Here is what it means in plain English.

Password entropy sounds like a topic for mathematicians, but it is really a simple idea: it measures how many guesses an attacker would need, on average, to find your password by trying every possibility. It is measured in bits, and each extra bit doubles the work. A password with 40 bits of entropy takes about a trillion guesses to crack; one with 80 bits takes so many guesses that no computer on Earth could finish in your lifetime. Understanding entropy helps you judge whether a password is actually strong, instead of relying on a website's password meter, which may be misleading.

Entropy in plain English

Imagine a combination lock. A lock with two dials, each numbered 0 to 9, has 100 possible combinations. Add a third dial and there are 1,000. Entropy works the same way: it counts the total number of possible passwords in the recipe you used. If your password is 8 characters drawn from 26 lowercase letters, there are 26 to the power of 8 possibilities, which is about 209 billion — and that is about 37 bits of entropy. The formula is simple: entropy equals the length multiplied by the logarithm of the character-set size. You do not need to calculate it by hand; our entropy calculator on this site does it for you. The key insight is that both length and character variety matter, but length matters more.

How many bits do you actually need?

As a rough guide, under 40 bits is weak and can be cracked quickly by a well-equipped attacker. Around 50 bits is moderate — fine for a low-value account but not for email or banking. Around 60 to 70 bits is good for most purposes, and 80 bits or more is strong enough that brute force is effectively impossible. For comparison, a random 12-character password using upper and lower case letters, digits and symbols has about 78 bits, and a random 16-character password of the same kind has about 105 bits. A passphrase of six random words has about 78 bits too. These numbers assume the attacker must guess blindly; if the password is a dictionary word or a common pattern, its real entropy is far lower than the formula suggests.

Why password meters can mislead you

Many websites show a strength meter when you type a password, but the meter usually checks only simple rules: length, character types, and a list of common passwords. It cannot tell whether your password is a line from a song, your pet's name plus a birth year, or a word that appears in a leaked password list. A password like P@ssw0rd! may score as strong because it has upper and lower case, digits and a symbol, yet it appears in every password-cracking dictionary and would fall in seconds. Real entropy depends on how unpredictable the password is to an attacker who knows your habits, not on which character types it contains. Treat meters as a rough hint, not a verdict.

Length beats complexity, almost always

Adding one character to a password multiplies the guessing work by the size of the character set, while adding a symbol type increases it only slightly. Going from 12 to 16 characters adds far more entropy than switching from letters-only to letters-plus-symbols at the same length. This is why a long passphrase of plain words can beat a short jumble of symbols, and why the most important upgrade you can make is usually to make the password longer. Complexity still helps, especially against attackers who assume passwords are only letters, but it is no substitute for length. If you remember one rule, make it this: longer is stronger.

Using entropy to compare password choices

Entropy gives you a fair way to compare options. A four-word passphrase at about 52 bits is weaker than a 12-character random password at about 78 bits, but stronger than an 8-character random password at about 52 bits — roughly equal, in fact. A six-word passphrase at about 78 bits matches the 12-character random password while being far easier to remember. When a site forces a maximum length of 12 characters, use every character type available to squeeze the most entropy from those 12 characters. And when a site allows a long passphrase, take the full length. Entropy is not the whole story — uniqueness and two-factor authentication matter just as much — but it is the best single number for judging a password's raw strength.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good entropy number for a password?

Aim for at least 60 bits for ordinary accounts and 80 bits or more for email, banking and your password manager.

Does adding a symbol make my password much stronger?

It helps a little, but adding extra characters helps far more. Length is the biggest lever you have.

Can I trust a website's password strength meter?

Only roughly. Meters cannot detect dictionary words, song lyrics or personal details, so a high score does not guarantee a strong password.
