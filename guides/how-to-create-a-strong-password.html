How to Create a Strong Password That You Can Actually Remember

A practical method for building passwords that are long, random and memorable — without writing them on a sticky note.

A strong password is the cheapest security upgrade you can make, and it costs nothing but a few minutes of thought. Most accounts are broken into not because the attacker is a genius, but because the password was short, reused, or easy to guess. A good password does three things at once: it is long enough to resist guessing, random enough to resist dictionaries, and unique to one account so a breach somewhere else cannot reach you. The trick is building all three qualities into something you can actually use every day, because a password you cannot remember will be written down, reset constantly, or quietly weakened. This guide walks through a method that works for real people, not just security experts.

Start with length, not cleverness

Length is the single biggest factor in password strength. Every character you add multiplies the number of combinations an attacker must try, so a 16-character password is enormously stronger than an 8-character one, even if the shorter one looks clever. Security researchers generally recommend at least 12 characters for ordinary accounts and 16 or more for important ones like email and banking. Do not worry about making the password look complicated with leetspeak substitutions like replacing E with 3; attackers know those tricks and try them first. A long, plain phrase that only you would construct beats a short string of symbols every time, and it is far easier to type correctly on a phone keyboard.

Use a passphrase you can picture

One of the easiest ways to get length is a passphrase: four or five random words strung together, such as correct-horse-battery-staple. The words do not need to make sense together; in fact, nonsense is better, because a sentence from a book or a song lyric can be guessed from published text. Pick words by rolling dice, using a generator, or opening a book at random pages and pointing. Then add a number or a symbol somewhere in the middle rather than at the end, since attackers expect digits at the end. If you can picture the words as a strange little scene — a purple bicycle parked on a roof — you will remember the phrase far more easily than a random jumble of letters.

Make every password unique

Reusing one good password across many sites is like using one key for your house, car, office and safe: convenient until it is copied. Data breaches happen constantly, and stolen passwords are traded in bulk. If your password for a shopping site is the same as your email password, one breach hands an attacker the keys to everything, including password-reset emails. You do not need to memorise twenty unique passwords by hand. Use a password manager to generate and store one strong password per site, and memorise only the manager's master password. That single password should be your longest and strongest — a six-word passphrase is a good choice.

Store it safely, not on paper

Writing passwords on sticky notes, in a notebook by the computer, or in an unprotected phone note defeats the purpose. Anyone who can see the note can use the password, and notes get photographed, thrown away or found. A reputable password manager encrypts your passwords behind one master password and fills them in for you, so you rarely need to type them at all. If you truly distrust software, a written list kept in a locked drawer at home is better than reusing passwords, but it cannot be backed up, searched, or used when you travel. Whatever you choose, never email a password to yourself and never store passwords in a plain spreadsheet.

Turn on two-factor authentication

Even the best password can be stolen by phishing or a breach, so add a second factor wherever it is offered. An authenticator app on your phone is the strongest common option, followed by a hardware security key, with text-message codes as a weaker but still useful fallback. Two-factor authentication means a stolen password alone is not enough to get in. Set it up first on your email account, because email is the key to resetting almost everything else. Then add it to banking, social media and cloud storage. It takes a few minutes per account and is the highest-value security step after unique passwords.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a password be?

Aim for at least 12 characters for everyday accounts and 16 or more for email, banking and password-manager master passwords.

Is a passphrase really safe?

Yes, if the words are random and there are enough of them. Four random words from a large list give roughly 50 bits of entropy, and five or six words are stronger still.

What if I forget my new password?

Use the account's reset process, then store the new password in a password manager immediately so you do not have to memorise it.
