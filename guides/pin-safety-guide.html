PIN Safety: How to Choose and Protect a PIN

Four digits guard your phone, your bank card and your front door. Choose them well and keep them secret.

A PIN is a short numeric password, usually four to six digits, and it protects some of the most valuable things you own: your phone, your bank card, and increasingly your front door. Because a PIN is so short, it has far fewer combinations than a password — a four-digit PIN has only ten thousand possibilities — so the rules for choosing one are different. A PIN is safe mainly because the device locks after a few wrong guesses, not because the number itself is unguessable. That makes your choice of digits and your habits around the PIN more important than they might seem.

Why birthdays and 1234 are terrible PINs

Attackers know the most common PINs, and they try them first. Studies of leaked PINs show that 1234, 1111, 0000, 1212 and birth years dominate the list, and a handful of guesses covers a large share of all PINs in use. Your birth year, your children's birth years, your house number and your phone number are all discoverable from social media, documents and data brokers, so they are the first things an attacker tries. The same applies to repeating patterns on a keypad, like 2580 down the middle column. A good PIN is random: use a generator, pick digits that mean nothing to you, and avoid any sequence, repeated digit, or date connected to your life.

How many digits should your PIN have?

Four digits are the minimum most systems allow, and six digits are much better — a six-digit PIN has a million combinations instead of ten thousand. If your phone or bank offers a six-digit or longer PIN, take it. Some systems allow alphanumeric passcodes, which are stronger still, and you should use one for your phone if you can, since the phone guards your email, banking and photos. For a bank card, the PIN length is set by the bank, but you can still choose random digits. For a smart lock, use a different PIN from your phone and card, and change it when someone who had it no longer needs access, such as a former cleaner or tenant.

Protecting your PIN in public

The most common way a PIN is stolen is not hacking; it is someone watching. Cover the keypad with your free hand whenever you type a PIN at a cash machine or a shop terminal, and shield your phone screen when unlocking it on a bus or in a queue. Be aware of cameras, including the phone cameras of people standing behind you, and of reflections in windows and mirrors. Never say your PIN out loud, never type it while someone is looking over your shoulder, and never let a shop assistant or a stranger type it for you. If you think someone saw your PIN, change it immediately — it takes a minute at a cash machine or in your phone settings.

What to do if your PIN is compromised

If your card is lost or stolen, call your bank at once to block it; do not wait to see whether money disappears. If someone learned your phone PIN, change it immediately and check for unfamiliar activity, such as new apps, changed settings or messages you did not send. If a smart-lock PIN was shared with someone who should no longer have it, change the code and, if the lock supports it, check its access log. After any compromise, also change any password that was protected by the same PIN — for example, if your phone unlock PIN was also the code for your banking app, change the banking credentials too. Quick action limits the damage to minutes rather than days.

Managing several PINs without confusion

You need different PINs for your phone, your bank card and your home, and remembering several random numbers is genuinely hard. A password manager can store PINs as secure notes, so you only need to remember your master password. Alternatively, keep a coded hint that only you understand — never the PIN itself written plainly. Some people use a pattern they can reconstruct, such as the second letters of a phrase converted to digits, but make sure the method itself is not guessable from your life. Whatever system you use, never write a PIN on the card itself, on a note in your wallet, or in your phone's contacts under a fake name — thieves look for exactly those things.

Frequently asked questions

Is 1234 really that bad?

Yes. It is the most common PIN in existence, and attackers try it first. A random PIN is far safer.

Should I use a 6-digit PIN instead of 4?

Yes, if your device allows it. Six digits have a hundred times more combinations than four.

Can I use the same PIN for my phone and my bank card?

No. Use a different PIN for each, so one compromised PIN does not unlock everything.
