Password Length vs Complexity: What Matters More?

Symbols, numbers, capitals — or just more characters? The maths says one of them wins by a wide margin.

Password advice often sounds contradictory: one site demands a symbol and a number, another demands sixteen characters, and a third rejects your password for reasons it will not explain. Behind the confusion is a real question with a clear answer: when you have to choose, is it better to make a password longer, or to make it more complex? The mathematics is unambiguous, and it has changed how security experts write the rules. This guide explains the trade-off, why length usually wins, and when complexity still matters.

The maths in one paragraph

Password strength is measured by the number of possible combinations, and combinations grow in two ways: a bigger character set, or a longer password. Adding a new character type — say, symbols — multiplies the possibilities by a modest factor. Adding one more character multiplies them by the size of the whole set. Concretely, going from 10 to 12 characters multiplies the guessing work by roughly ten thousand, while adding symbols to a 10-character password multiplies it by less than ten. Length wins because each extra character compounds, while complexity only adds once. That is why a 16-character password of plain lowercase letters is far stronger than an 8-character password using every character type.

Why complexity rules backfired

For years, websites enforced complexity: at least one upper-case letter, one digit, one symbol. The intention was good, but the result was predictable human behaviour: people took a weak word and added a capital at the start, a digit at the end, and an exclamation mark after it — Password1! — and attackers learned to try exactly those patterns first. Complexity rules also made passwords harder to remember, which pushed people toward reuse and writing passwords down. Modern guidance has shifted: instead of demanding character types, the best sites require a minimum length and check the password against lists of leaked and common passwords. Length plus a breach check beats complexity rules in practice.

When complexity still helps

Complexity is not useless. A password drawn from a larger character set has more entropy per character, which matters when length is limited — for example, a site that caps passwords at 12 characters, or a PIN pad that allows only digits. Complexity also helps against attackers who assume passwords are only letters, and it makes dictionary attacks less effective. The right way to think about it is that complexity is a bonus on top of length, not a substitute for it. If you can have both, take both: a long password that also uses several character types is stronger than either quality alone. But if you must choose, choose length every time.

What this means for passphrases

The length-versus-complexity debate is exactly why passphrases work. A passphrase of five ordinary words is long — often 25 characters or more — but uses only lowercase letters and a separator, so it looks simple to a complexity checker. Yet its entropy is high, because each word adds about 13 bits. A complexity-obsessed meter might rate it poorly, while a length-aware one rates it highly. This is a good reminder to judge passwords by their entropy and their randomness, not by how many character types they contain. If a site's rules force you to add a digit or symbol to a passphrase, add it in the middle rather than at the end, where attackers expect it.

A practical rule for every account

Aim for at least 16 characters on important accounts, using all the character types the site allows, generated by a password manager so you never have to remember it. Where you must remember the password yourself, use a passphrase of five or six random words. Do not shorten a password to satisfy a complexity rule, and do not add complexity to a short password and call it strong. The hierarchy is simple: first make it long, then make it random, then add variety, then make it unique, then protect it with two-factor authentication. Follow that order and you will spend your effort where it actually makes a difference.

Frequently asked questions

Is a 20-character lowercase password strong?

Yes, very. Twenty lowercase letters have about 94 bits of entropy — far stronger than an 8-character password with symbols.

Should I use symbols in my passwords?

Yes, when you can — they add entropy. But adding two more characters adds more strength than adding a symbol type.

Why do sites still demand a symbol?

Many sites use old rules. Meet the requirement, but do not rely on it: length and randomness matter far more.
