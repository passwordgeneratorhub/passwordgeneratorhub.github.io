10 Common Password Mistakes (and How to Fix Them)

Most account break-ins trace back to a handful of avoidable habits. Check how many of these you still do.

Most accounts are not broken into by brilliant hackers defeating clever defences. They are broken into because the owner made one of a handful of common mistakes: a short password, a reused password, a guessable password, or a password given away to a fake login page. The good news is that these mistakes are easy to fix, and fixing them removes the vast majority of the risk most people face. Work through this list honestly, fix the items that apply to you, and your accounts will be far safer than the average person's within a day.

Mistake 1: using short passwords

An eight-character password can be brute-forced by a fast computer in hours, and a six-character one in minutes. Short passwords survive only because attackers usually try dictionaries and leaked lists first, not brute force — but once they do try brute force, short passwords fall immediately. The fix is simple: make every important password at least 12 characters, and 16 where you can. Length is the cheapest strength you can buy, and a password manager makes long passwords painless because you never have to type or remember them.

Mistake 2: reusing one password everywhere

Reusing a password means one breach compromises every account that shares it, and breaches happen constantly. Attackers automate the process, testing stolen passwords against hundreds of sites within minutes of a leak. The fix is uniqueness: a different password for every account, stored in a password manager. If that feels like too much work, start with email, banking and cloud storage — the three accounts that guard all the rest — and work outward from there.

Mistake 3: using personal details

Names of partners, children and pets, birth dates, anniversaries, hometowns and favourite teams are all guessable, and much of this information is visible on social media or public records. Attackers trying to break into a specific person's account try personal details first. The fix is randomness: generate passwords rather than inventing them, and never use a word that appears on your social media profiles. If a site forces you to set security questions, treat the answers as extra passwords — answer with random words stored in your manager, not with true facts anyone can look up.

Mistake 4: predictable patterns and substitutions

Keyboard walks like qwerty and 123456, and substitutions like P@ssw0rd, are in every cracking dictionary. Attackers have tried every variation of these patterns millions of times. The fix is to stop trying to be clever and start being random: use a generator for passwords and random word lists for passphrases. Randomness is not something humans are good at producing by hand, so let a tool do it.

Mistakes 5 to 10, and the fixes

Mistake five is writing passwords on sticky notes or in unprotected phone notes; use a password manager instead. Mistake six is sharing passwords by email, chat or text; use the manager's secure sharing feature or change the password after sharing. Mistake seven is ignoring two-factor authentication; turn it on for email and banking first. Mistake eight is staying logged in on shared computers; always sign out and never save passwords on a device you do not own. Mistake nine is using the same password for years after a breach; check breach alerts and change affected passwords the same day. Mistake ten is trusting a password strength meter blindly; a long random password beats a short one that scores well. Fix these ten and you have removed the easiest paths into your accounts.

Frequently asked questions

What is the single worst password mistake?

Reusing one password across many accounts, because a single breach then opens everything at once.

Is writing passwords down always bad?

A sticky note on your monitor is bad, but a password manager is the right way to store them, and a written master-password backup kept at home is sensible.

How do I fix all of these at once?

Install a password manager, change your email and banking passwords first, turn on two-factor authentication, then work through the rest over a few weeks.
